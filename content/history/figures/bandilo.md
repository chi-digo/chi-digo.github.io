# Bandilo: The Diviner-Leader of the Wadigo

## Overview

**Era:** Pre-colonial (date unknown)
**Role:** Mtawala (leader) remembered as an archer, healer and diviner
**Region:** Tanga, present-day Tanzania

---

Among the Wadigo of the Tanga coast, one early leader is remembered by name: Bandilo. A short Swahili account of Wadigo history, retold on Tanzanian websites and social media, describes him as a ruler (*mtawala*) whose standing came from his skill with the bow, his knowledge of medicine and his gift for foretelling events. Everything we can say about him comes from that one account, so this article keeps close to its words.

## The Archer and the Healer

The account says Bandilo was greatly respected because he was *hodari katika matumizi ya mishale*, skilled in the use of arrows. He also had *utaalamu wa dawa za asili*, expertise in traditional medicine, and could *kutabiri matukio mbalimbali*, predict all kinds of events.

Archery fits what outside observers saw in the Digo country of Tanga. The German geographer Oscar Baumann travelled through it in 1890. He noted that even in villages a few hours from the coast, men carried bows and reed arrows with barbed iron heads, and that guns were rare. A leader praised for archery was praised for the skill on which his people's defence depended.

Healing and divination belonged to the same world. Among the Digo, as among other Mijikenda, the *mganga* treated illness and read the causes of misfortune, which were often thought to be spiritual as well as physical. The account links Bandilo's authority to this role. It also says the Wadigo believed in the spirits of their ancestors, called *Madibwe* or *Adibwe*, who after death gained the power to protect or to trouble the living.

## A Leader Others Consulted

According to the account, Bandilo's reputation reached beyond his own people: *"Kazi ya utabiri na utabibu ilisababisha mtawala huyo kuheshimiwa pia na watawala wa makabila mengine ambao walifika kwake kufanya utabiri na kutafuta suluhu za matatizo mbalimbali yanayohitaji maamuzi magumu."* His work as diviner and healer earned him the respect of rulers of other peoples, who came to him for divination and for answers to problems that needed hard decisions.

The account does not say which peoples these were. The Wadigo of Tanga lived among the Bondei, Segeju and Shambaa, and Baumann records feuds and wars with all three. In those wars the Digo usually beat the Bondei but usually lost to the Segeju and to the Shambaa under Hungura. Bondei tradition remembers a war with the Digo, the Milonge war, which a Tanzanian historian dates to the late 17th or early 18th century. A diviner whom neighbouring rulers trusted would have mattered in a region with this much conflict, but the sources do not tell us which rulers came to Bandilo, or when.

## The Day of Peace

The same account describes a ritual for peace. To strengthen peace in the community, it says, their ruler "known as Bandiwe" set aside a special day (*siku maalumu*) for a *tambiko*, an offering and prayer asking for peace and blessings. On that day he wore formal dress and led a prayer, followed by a great celebration. People gathered carrying hoes and spears, then ate, drank and danced to the drums.

The spelling here is *Bandiwe*, not *Bandilo*. This is most likely the same leader under a variant or misspelt name, but the account does not say so. The account does not explain the hoes and spears. They were the everyday tools of farming and of defence, carried to a ceremony that asked for peace and blessing.

## Why the Record Is Thin

Bandilo's story survives in a single short Swahili text. It appears almost word for word on a Tanzanian blog (2017), on a Tanga community page and in a JamiiForums thread on Wadigo history. No author is named, and no elder is quoted. The text cites Martin Kayamba's "Notes on the Wadigo", published in *Tanganyika Notes and Records* in 1947, which may be where the story of Bandilo was first written down. That article has not yet been checked. Vincent Nkondokaya's book on the origins of the Tanga Wadigo (2006) is also said to mention Bandilo, but no copy has yet been read for this project. The same online text also makes claims that historians reject, such as placing Shungwaya in West Africa. So it is a weak guide to detail, even if the memory of Bandilo is genuine.

Baumann, the main German writer on the Tanga Digo, does not mention Bandilo. He describes a people whose villages were "not under chiefs but governed by a few elders". Now and then one elder rose to special standing, such as the blind headman of the village of Zirihini or the feared leader Jambia. A true chief over all the Wadigo, Jumbe Mamfulla of Gombelo, was first installed by the German authorities. On the Kenyan side, each Digo kaya was governed by a council of elders, the *ngambi*, led by a senior elder (*mwanatsi*) from the founding matrilineal clan. In a society governed this way, a single remembered "ruler" stands out. It also means power was shared, and individual leaders were rarely written into the record.

No source dates Bandilo, names his village or his clan (*fuko*), or records his deeds beyond those described above.

## Legacy

Bandilo is remembered for three things: the bow, medicine and foresight. He is also remembered for a day set aside to pray for peace. The memory of him as someone whom neighbouring rulers came to for counsel offers a picture of leadership that is still valued among the Wadigo: authority earned through knowledge and trust. Finding out more about him is one of the open tasks of Digo history. Kayamba's notes, Nkondokaya's book and the recollections of Tanga elders may yet tell us when he lived, where he led and how his name has been handed down.
